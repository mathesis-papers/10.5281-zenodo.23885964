A negative answer to Kang–Jäger's Question 1.10 on permutative lifts

Haobo Ma, Wenlin Zhang

Paper

A local rule can transform a space of infinite symbol sequences into another such space; reversing that rule creates a larger system of possible inputs. Haobo Ma and Wenlin Zhang construct one highly orderly but nonperiodic binary system for which every rule of the specified kind produces a collection of separate dynamical copies, rather than one system whose trajectories explore everything. Their answer is negative even when the original system has both dense trajectories and uniquely determined long-run statistics.

The sequences extend infinitely in both directions, and time advances by shifting every symbol one place. A closed collection preserved by this shift is a subshift. It is minimal when every trajectory is dense, and strictly ergodic when it is also governed by exactly one shift-invariant probability measure. A local rule reads a fixed-length window and outputs one bit. Here “permutative” means that changing either endpoint, while fixing every other input, changes the output bijectively. Kang and Jäger asked whether every minimal or strictly ergodic base admits some such rule, of length at least two, whose entire inverse image retains that property. Selecting just one well-behaved component is not enough.

The authors’ answer uses a single base that defeats all lengths and all these rules simultaneously. For a window length ℓ, the entire inverse image is continuously and reversibly identifiable with 2^(ℓ−1) separate copies of the base: shifting advances the base coordinate but leaves the copy label fixed. Each copy is both open and closed and is strictly ergodic. Thus the whole lift is neither minimal nor uniquely ergodic; its invariant measures are precisely weighted mixtures of the copies’ unique measures. Kang and Jäger had already obtained failures with a base chosen for each fixed rule, and a failing rule for each Toeplitz base. The advance is choosing the base before every rule and length.

The construction builds two equal-length words at each level, starting with U₀ = 0 and V₀ = 1. To form the next pair, choose the repetition count m = (n+1)!: the first new word consists of twice m copies of Uₙ followed by m copies of Vₙ, and the second uses m copies of Uₙ followed by twice m copies of Vₙ. The base consists of sequences admitting such tilings at every level. Initially the words are 001 and 011: only their middle positions differ. The transition 10 marks boundaries between these words. The same marker pattern, interpreted as transitions between lower-level tile types, makes every higher-level tiling uniquely recognizable.

These tiles explain the base’s orderliness. Every short word that occurs eventually appears inside every sufficiently large tile, giving bounded gaps and hence minimality. Recognizable tile positions also exclude periodic sequences: a global period would have to be divisible by the unbounded tile lengths. At level n, the proportion of positions where the two tile types disagree is exactly 3^(−n). An aligned sequence has every coordinate repeating periodically, although no single period covers the whole sequence; this is the Toeplitz property. The density of its periodic positions tends to one, making it regular. Finally, invariant measures assign equal mass to every tile position. A fixed observation window can distinguish measures only near disagreement positions, whose proportion tends to zero, proving unique ergodicity.

Why do all lifts split? Remembering the first ℓ−1 input bits turns inversion of a rule into a finite-state process. Each output bit permutes these states: one endpoint condition determines the next bit, and the other makes the transition reversible. The authors credit this representation to Kang and Jäger. More generally, assign any two permutations of any finite state set to the binary letters. At sufficiently high levels, factorial repetition makes each repeated block act as the identity, because every permutation’s order divides the factorial of the number of states. Consequently both whole tiles act trivially, without requiring the permutations to commute. Relabel a state using the inverse accumulated action from its tile’s beginning. This label remains unchanged under shifting, including at boundaries, where the whole-tile action is identity. Recognizability makes the relabeling continuous, yielding the claimed copies. Earlier work by Berthé and collaborators supplies the broader obstruction framework.

The scope is an exact, all-length mathematical claim supported here by a written argument, not merely a finite search. The note reports finite checks of initial construction levels and all binary permutative rules of lengths two through five; those checks do not establish the universal statement. Its stated Lean evidence consists of two declarations negating universal existence claims, including the regular Toeplitz strictly ergodic case; the full conjugacy, component count and measure description are supplied as written proofs. The authors thereby settle the universal question negatively, including its Toeplitz variants, while leaving Kang and Jäger’s positive results for other bases intact. This does not characterize which other bases permit minimal lifts.
